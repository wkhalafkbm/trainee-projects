## About This Document

Many customers have questions about the Islamic principles behind our products — not just how the products work, but why they are structured the way they are, and whether they are truly Sharia-compliant. These are important questions and we welcome them.

This FAQ is prepared jointly by our Sharia Supervisory Board Secretariat and our Customer Experience team. The answers reflect the position of our Sharia Board on the questions most commonly raised by customers.

> **Disclaimer:** This FAQ is intended to address common questions in plain language and provide general guidance on Islamic finance principles as applied to our products. It does not constitute a formal fatwa (religious ruling) or Sharia opinion. For complex personal circumstances — such as inheritance planning, business structures with Sharia implications, or detailed questions about the permissibility of specific transactions — we strongly recommend consulting a qualified Islamic scholar. Our Sharia Board does not provide individual customer consultations, but can recommend recognized scholars upon request.

### Q1: Are your products actually halal? How do I know?

Yes, all financing products offered by Al-Baraka Islamic Bank have been reviewed and approved by our independent Sharia Supervisory Board before being offered to customers.

The Board is composed of recognized Islamic scholars who are qualified in both Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh al-muamalat — the area of Islamic law governing financial transactions) and modern finance. Their approval is not a formality — it is a substantive review of the contract structure, the flow of funds, and the obligations of each party.

Specifically, the Board has issued written fatwas approving our Murabaha, Ijara, and Tawarruq products. These fatwas are on file and available for review upon request at any branch. In addition, the Board conducts an annual Sharia audit to confirm that approved products continue to be implemented as approved — not just on paper, but in actual practice.

The bank is also supervised by the Central Bank of Kuwait (CBK), which has a dedicated Islamic banking supervision framework. CBK examiners review the bank's Sharia governance as part of their regular supervisory activities.

### Q2: What is the Sharia Supervisory Board? Who are its members?

The Sharia Supervisory Board (SSB) is an independent body of Islamic scholars who oversee the bank's compliance with Sharia principles. The Board:

- Reviews and approves new products before launch
- Conducts annual Sharia audits of existing products and operations
- Issues fatwas on novel financial questions
- Reviews customer complaints that have a Sharia dimension
- Publishes an annual Sharia compliance report included in the bank's annual report

**Independence:** Board members are not employees of the bank and do not receive performance-linked compensation. Their fees are fixed and independent of the bank's profitability. This independence is essential to the credibility of their oversight.

**Qualifications:** Each Board member holds a recognized academic qualification in Islamic jurisprudence (typically a doctorate in fiqh al-muamalat or Islamic economics) and has practical experience in Islamic finance. The Board currently has three members. Their full names, academic credentials, and institutional affiliations are published in the annual Sharia compliance report.

The CBK requires all Islamic banks operating in Kuwait to have a Sharia Supervisory Board that meets minimum qualification and independence standards (CBK Circular No. 2/BS/342/2013).

### Q3: Is this just a conventional loan with Islamic labels? How is it really different?

This is one of the most important questions a customer can ask. The honest answer is: the difference is structural and legal, and it is real — though it is true that some critics argue that certain Islamic finance structures are economically similar to conventional loans.

Here is how our products genuinely differ:

**Murabaha:** In a conventional car loan, the bank gives you money and you repay more money over time (interest). In Murabaha, the bank actually buys the car — it becomes the owner of the car for a brief moment. The bank then sells the car to you at a higher price. The bank's return comes from a trade transaction, not from lending money. The legal structure is genuinely different.

**Ijara:** In a conventional mortgage, the bank lends you money secured against the property. You own the property from day one but owe the bank money. If the property burns down and is uninsured, that is your problem — the bank still wants its money back. In Ijara, the bank owns the property. If the property suffers catastrophic loss through no fault of the customer, the bank bears that loss as the property owner. This is a meaningful difference in legal risk allocation.

**Tawarruq:** This is the product where critics make the strongest argument, because the commodity transaction is brief and the economic result is similar to a personal loan. Our Sharia Board has reviewed Tawarruq carefully. Their position is that the commodity transactions are real (they happen on actual commodity markets with real buyers and sellers), the structure meets the technical requirements of Islamic jurisprudence, and the product is permissible. However, some Islamic scholars hold more conservative views. We recommend speaking with a scholar if you have concerns.

The Islamic Finance Council and AAOIFI (Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions) have published standards on each of these product types. Our products are designed to comply with AAOIFI standards.

### Q4: What is the difference between Murabaha and a conventional car loan?

| Feature | Al-Baraka Murabaha | Conventional Car Loan |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Bank buys car, sells to customer at markup | Bank lends money; customer buys car |
| Bank's return | Disclosed profit margin (from trade) | Interest (from lending money) |
| Amount you pay | Fixed from day one — never changes | May vary if variable rate; fixed if fixed rate |
| Who owns the car at contract signing? | Customer (after Murabaha sale) | Customer (purchased with loan funds) |
| What if you pay early? | Bank may offer ibra (rebate) — never a penalty | May have prepayment penalty (conventional) |
| Cost transparency | Bank discloses its purchase price and profit | Interest rate disclosed, but total cost less obvious over time |
| Sharia status | Permissible (our Sharia Board ruling) | Contains riba — not permissible under Islamic law |

In both cases, you end up owning a car and making monthly payments. The difference is in the legal structure of the transaction and the nature of the bank's profit. Islamic finance holds that how profit is made matters, not just the economic outcome.

### Q6: Can I pay off my financing early? Is there a penalty?

You may settle your financing early at any time. **There is no penalty for early settlement.** Charging a penalty for early repayment would itself be a form of riba (charging extra money for the time value of money) and is therefore prohibited under Islamic principles.

When you settle early, you pay the outstanding principal amount. The bank may, at its discretion, grant an **ibra** — a reduction of the remaining profit that has not yet been earned. This is a voluntary act of good faith by the bank and is not contractually guaranteed.

Our Sharia Board's position is that granting ibra on early settlement is encouraged as a matter of fairness, and the bank has a policy of applying ibra in most early settlement cases. The ibra calculation is based on the proportion of the financing term remaining at the time of settlement.

To calculate an early settlement amount, contact any branch or call 1800-456.

### Q7: What happens if I miss a payment?

We understand that financial circumstances can change. If you anticipate difficulty making a payment, please contact us as soon as possible — before the payment is missed, if possible. We have a customer hardship process and may be able to restructure your installment schedule.

If a payment is missed:
1. The bank will contact you (by phone and written notice) to inform you of the missed payment and ask you to bring the account up to date.
2. A grace period applies before any formal action is taken.
3. If the account remains in arrears, the bank will work with you to assess the situation and explore options.

For Ijara (property financing), persistent arrears can ultimately result in the bank exercising its right to repossess the property (which it legally owns during the financing period) and sell it to recover the outstanding amount. This is a last resort and the bank is required to follow CBK regulations on consumer finance defaults before taking such action.

### Q8: Is there a late payment charge? I heard Islamic banks cannot charge interest on overdue payments.

This is correct — Islamic banks cannot charge interest on late payments, because charging additional money for the passage of time is riba, which is prohibited.

However, to encourage timely payment and to cover genuine administrative costs, the bank charges a nominal **late payment administrative fee** of KWD 5 per missed installment. This fee is fixed — it does not compound or increase over time. It is not interest.

Additionally, customers who are in persistent arrears may be required to make a **charitable donation** to a bank-selected charity as a condition of account rehabilitation. This practice is known in Islamic finance as ta'widh (compensation) and gharamah (penalty donation). The charitable donation goes entirely to charity — the bank does not retain it as income. This distinguishes it from conventional interest, where the financial institution retains the penalty as profit.

Our Sharia Board has approved both the late payment fee and the charitable donation mechanism as Sharia-compliant approaches to late payment management.

### Q9: If I cannot repay my Ijara financing and the bank takes the property, will I owe anything more?

If the bank repossesses and sells the property, the proceeds from the sale are applied to your outstanding obligation. If the sale proceeds are sufficient to cover the outstanding balance (plus reasonable recovery costs), the matter is settled and you have no further obligation to the bank.

If the sale proceeds fall short of your outstanding balance (for example, if property values have fallen significantly), you remain liable for the shortfall under the terms of the financing contract. The bank will work with you to agree a repayment arrangement for any shortfall.

Any surplus from the property sale (proceeds exceeding your outstanding balance) will be returned to you.

### Q10: What is the difference between Ijara and a conventional mortgage?

| Feature | Al-Baraka Ijara | Conventional Mortgage |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the property? | The bank (until final payment) | The customer (from day one, mortgaged to bank) |
| Nature of payment | Rent (Ijara) + gradual ownership transfer | Loan repayment + interest |
| Bank's return | Rental income (from lease) | Interest income (from lending) |
| Variable rate risk | Fixed in most structures | Can be variable (interest rate risk) |
| Insurance requirement | Takaful (Islamic insurance) | Conventional insurance or Takaful |
| If property is destroyed | Risk shared per ownership proportion | Customer's loss; loan still owed |
| Early settlement penalty | None (ibra may be granted) | May apply in conventional products |

### Q11: Is Tawarruq really halal? I have heard scholars disagree about it.

You have raised a genuine and important point. Tawarruq is a topic of ongoing scholarly debate in Islamic finance, and not all scholars agree that contemporary organized Tawarruq (as used in banking) is permissible.

The disagreement, broadly:
- **Scholars who permit Tawarruq** (including the majority of Kuwaiti and GCC scholars, and AAOIFI standards): The commodity transactions are real. Real metals change hands on real markets. The structure satisfies the technical requirements of bay' al-muajjal (deferred-price sale). The permissibility of the transaction should be judged on its structure, not its economic similarity to a loan.
- **Scholars who are more cautious or who restrict Tawarruq:** When the commodity transactions happen near-simultaneously and the customer never intends to actually hold the commodity, the transaction may be seen as a legal fiction — a hiyal (legal stratagem) to achieve what is economically a loan. Some scholars, particularly associated with the Hanbali tradition, take a stricter view.

**Our Sharia Board's position:** Our Board has reviewed organized Tawarruq in detail and issued a fatwa permitting its use within the structure we employ, subject to specific conditions (real commodity transactions, use of a genuine third-party buyer, no pre-arrangement between parties). We follow these conditions strictly.

We want to be transparent about the scholarly debate rather than pretend there is no disagreement. If you have personal religious concerns about Tawarruq, we respect that and encourage you to seek guidance from a scholar whose opinion you trust. We are happy to provide you with the references our Board relied upon.

### Q13: Does the Sharia Board approve individual customer transactions, or just the product templates?

In most cases, the Sharia Board approves the product structure and the standard contract templates. Individual transactions that fall within these approved templates do not require separate Board approval — they are covered by the existing fatwa.

For unusual or large transactions (such as an Ijara for a non-standard asset, or a complex Murabaha involving imported goods), the Sharia compliance team may refer the specific transaction to the Board for review before proceeding. Customers will be informed if this applies to their application.

### Q14: I have a more complex Sharia question about my personal financial situation. Can the Sharia Board advise me?

The Sharia Supervisory Board provides oversight of the bank's products and operations. It does not provide individual customer consultations or personal fatwa advice. Board members are scholars with significant professional obligations, and direct customer consultations are outside the scope of their role at the bank.

For complex personal Sharia questions — such as whether a particular transaction you are contemplating is permissible, or how to structure your finances in accordance with Islamic principles in your specific situation — we strongly recommend consulting a qualified Islamic scholar independently.

The Kuwait Awqaf Public Foundation (KAPF) and the Fatwa Department at the Ministry of Awqaf and Islamic Affairs can provide referrals to recognized Sharia scholars in Kuwait. Our customer service team can also provide a list of scholars our Sharia Board members have recommended for customer referrals, upon request.
