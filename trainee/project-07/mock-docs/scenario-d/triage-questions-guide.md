# Triage Questions Guide — Information Gathering at Intake
**Document ID:** HAL-TRIAGE-QUESTIONS-003
**Issued by:** Al-Hilal Private Hospital — Nursing and Clinical Operations
**Revision:** 1.8
**Last Updated:** March 2026
**Authorised by:** Director of Nursing; Chief of Clinical Operations

---

## Purpose

This guide defines the questions a triage assistant or intake system should ask to gather the information needed to classify a patient's urgency and route them to the correct department. It is not a clinical assessment guide. The goal of intake questioning is information collection, not diagnosis.

Ask questions clearly, at a pace the patient can follow. Avoid clinical jargon in communication with the patient. Use plain language. Never rush a patient through a question set in a way that prevents them from answering fully.

---

## Section 1 — Opening Questions

These questions are asked of every patient, in every case. They form the foundation of the intake record.

### 1.1 Chief Complaint

Ask: **"What brings you in today?"**

- Allow the patient to respond in their own words without interrupting for at least 30 seconds.
- Do not suggest answers or lead the patient toward a particular complaint.
- Record the patient's own words verbatim, then add the standardised complaint category.
- If the patient gives multiple complaints, ask: **"Which of these concerns you the most right now?"** Record all complaints but flag the primary one.

### 1.2 Duration

Ask: **"How long have you had this symptom / been feeling this way?"**

- If the answer is vague ("a while", "a few days"), follow up: **"Can you help me narrow it down — was it before or after the weekend?"** or use a recent event as a reference point.
- Record: hours / days / weeks / months.
- If the onset was sudden and the symptom is severe, this information must be escalated. Sudden onset of severe symptoms is a red flag regardless of the complaint type.

### 1.3 New or Recurring

Ask: **"Is this something you have experienced before, or is it completely new?"**

- If recurring: **"Is this episode the same as before, or does something feel different this time?"**
- A patient describing their usual chronic pain is generally lower acuity than the same patient saying "it's different this time." When a patient says a familiar symptom feels different, treat the complaint as new for triage purposes and apply red flag screening.

## Section 2 — Red Flag Screening

Red flag symptoms are specific presentations that require immediate escalation regardless of how the patient describes them or how calm the patient appears. These questions must be asked proactively if the patient has not already mentioned these symptoms. Do not wait for the patient to volunteer this information — many patients downplay or do not recognise the significance of their own symptoms.

Ask the following screening questions explicitly when they have not already been addressed in the opening questions:

### 2.1 Chest Pain

Ask: **"Do you have any pain, pressure, tightness, or discomfort in your chest?"**

- If yes, assign Immediate urgency. Notify the Emergency duty nurse verbally. Do not continue with the intake question set.
- Do not attempt to establish whether the pain is cardiac or non-cardiac. That is a clinical determination.

### 2.2 Difficulty Breathing

Ask: **"Are you having any difficulty breathing right now?"**

- If yes: **"Can you speak to me in full sentences without stopping to breathe?"**
- Any difficulty breathing at rest = Immediate urgency.
- Mild shortness of breath only on exertion in an otherwise stable patient = Urgent minimum.

### 2.3 Sudden Severe Headache

Ask: **"Have you had a sudden, severe headache — one that came on very quickly, within seconds?"**

- This is the classic description of a thunderclap headache, which may indicate a subarachnoid haemorrhage. It is distinct from a gradual-onset headache.
- If yes = Immediate or Urgent depending on current state. A patient with a resolved thunderclap headache must still be seen urgently — do not assume resolution means safety.

### 2.4 Loss of Consciousness

Ask: **"Have you fainted, blacked out, or lost consciousness at any point today, even briefly?"**

- If yes = Urgent at minimum. If the patient is currently confused, disoriented, or reports a period of unresponsiveness in the past 60 minutes = Immediate.
- Ask: **"Did anyone see what happened?"** A witness account of a seizure, collapse, or fall is clinically important — note the companion's name and contact details.

### 2.5 Stroke Symptoms — FAST Screening

Ask the following four questions if the patient has reported any of: sudden headache, facial symptoms, arm or leg weakness, speech difficulty, vision change, or dizziness:

- **Face:** "Has your face felt numb or drooped on one side at all today?"
- **Arms:** "Have you noticed any weakness or numbness in your arm or leg?"
- **Speech:** "Have you had any difficulty finding words, speaking, or understanding what people are saying to you?"
- **Time:** "When did these symptoms start?" (Time of onset is critical for treatment decisions.)

If any one FAST question is positive = Immediate urgency. Notify Emergency verbally. Note the exact time of symptom onset in the record — do not estimate; confirm with the patient or companion.

## Section 3 — Pain Assessment

### 3.1 Numeric Pain Scale

For all patients reporting pain, ask: **"On a scale of zero to ten, where zero is no pain at all and ten is the worst pain you can imagine, what number would you give your pain right now?"**

- Record the number as stated by the patient.
- Do not correct or challenge the patient's score. Pain is subjective.
- If the patient is unable to use a numeric scale (young child, cognitive impairment, language barrier), use the appropriate alternative: the Wong-Baker FACES scale for children, or a visual analogue scale for adults who struggle with numbers.

**Urgency guidance based on pain score:**
| Score | Urgency Guidance |
|---|---|
| 0 – 3 | Low pain — does not independently elevate urgency |
| 4 – 6 | Moderate pain — note and factor into overall assessment |
| 7 – 9 | Severe pain — elevate to Urgent if not already higher |
| 10 | Extreme pain — Urgent minimum; Immediate if combined with other red flags |

### 3.2 Pain Character

Ask: **"How would you describe the pain — is it sharp, dull, burning, cramping, or does it feel like pressure?"**

- Note the response. Crushing or pressure-type chest pain is particularly associated with cardiac events.
- Ask: **"Does the pain stay in one place, or does it move anywhere?"** Radiating pain (for example, pain spreading from the chest to the arm, jaw, or back) significantly increases the probability of a cardiac or vascular cause.

### 3.3 Aggravating and Relieving Factors

Ask: **"Does anything make it better or worse — movement, rest, food, position?"**

- Note the response without interpretation.

## Section 4 — Current Medications

Ask: **"Are you currently taking any medications, including prescribed medicines, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, or herbal supplements?"**

- If yes: **"Could you tell me the names of the medications, or do you have them with you?"** — Many patients carry a medications list or the actual boxes.
- If the patient cannot name their medications: note "patient on multiple medications, unable to name — companion/pharmacy record to be obtained."
- **Pay particular attention to:** blood thinners (warfarin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, aspirin), insulin or oral diabetes medications, immunosuppressants, cardiac medications (digoxin, beta-blockers, antiarrhythmics), psychiatric medications (lithium, antipsychotics, MAOIs), steroids.
- These medication classes are high-priority because they affect treatment decisions and indicate underlying conditions.

Ask separately: **"Do you have any known drug allergies?"**

- If yes: **"What happens when you take [the medication]?"** Document the reaction, not just the drug name. "Allergy to penicillin — rash" is more useful than "penicillin allergy."
- Allergies must be flagged in the patient record before the patient moves to any clinical area.

## Section 5 — Handling Distressed or Non-Communicating Patients

### 5.1 Patient in Visible Distress

A patient who is crying, agitated, in obvious pain, or too frightened to answer questions needs a modified approach:

1. **Do not proceed through the question list mechanically.** A distressed patient cannot provide reliable history, and delaying their triage to complete an intake form may cause harm.
2. **Prioritise the safety check first.** Ask only: "Are you having any chest pain?" and "Are you having trouble breathing?" — the two highest-risk automatic escalators. If either is present, escalate immediately.
3. **Move the patient to a calmer environment** if possible (a side room rather than the main waiting area) before continuing the intake.
4. **Speak slowly and calmly.** Introduce yourself by name. Use short sentences. Acknowledge the patient's distress explicitly: "I can see you are in a lot of pain. I want to help. I just need to ask you a few quick questions."
5. **Involve the companion** if the patient has one present. A companion may be able to fill in history that the patient cannot provide. Document that the history was provided by a companion and record their name.

### 5.2 Patient Who Cannot Communicate

For patients who cannot communicate due to unconsciousness, severe confusion, language barrier, speech or cognitive impairment, or extreme distress:

1. **If the patient is unconscious or unresponsive:** assign Immediate immediately and notify Emergency. Do not attempt to complete an intake form before escalating.
2. **If the patient has a language barrier:** use the hospital's medical interpreter service (internal extension 2150). Do not use family members as interpreters for clinical information — they may filter or misrepresent information and are not trained in medical terminology. Use a family member only to gather basic demographic information while the interpreter is being arranged.
3. **If the patient has cognitive impairment or communication difficulty:** speak directly to the patient using simple language and yes/no questions where possible. Supplement with information from any accompanying caregiver. Document the communication approach used.
4. **Never assume** a non-communicating patient is lower acuity. Unknown history in a patient who cannot communicate is itself a reason to escalate to a nurse for assessment.

## Section 6 — When the Complaint Has No Routing Rule

The department routing guide (HAL-TRIAGE-ROUTE-001) covers the most common presenting complaints. However, patients may describe symptoms that are unusual, highly specific, or that do not clearly match any routing pathway.

**When the triage assistant — human or system — does not have a routing rule for the complaint presented, the following applies without exception:**

1. **Do not attempt to improvise a routing decision.** An unmatched complaint is not the same as a low-acuity complaint. It means the triage system does not have enough information to make a safe decision.
2. **Assign a provisional urgency level based on the patient's overall appearance and any red flag symptoms.** If red flags are present, escalate immediately. If not, assign Semi-Urgent as a default.
3. **Refer the case to the senior triage nurse immediately.** Provide the senior nurse with: the patient's name and age, the complaint in the patient's words, any vitals taken, any red flags present or absent, and why the case did not match a routing rule.
4. **Document the escalation.** Note the time the case was referred to the nurse and the nurse's name.
5. **Stay with the patient** or ensure they are in a supervised waiting area until the nurse has assessed them. Do not direct an unclassified patient to wait in the general area and proceed to the next patient without completing the handover.

The senior triage nurse will make the routing and urgency decision for unmatched complaints. This is not a failure of the intake process — it is the system working correctly. Human clinical judgment exists precisely for situations the protocol does not cover.

## Quick Reference — Red Flag Question Checklist

Use this as a mental checklist during every patient intake. If the answer to any question is yes, escalate before continuing:

- [ ] Chest pain or pressure? (Immediate)
- [ ] Difficulty breathing at rest? (Immediate)
- [ ] Loss of consciousness today? (Immediate / Urgent)
- [ ] Sudden severe headache? (Immediate / Urgent)
- [ ] FAST stroke signs: face, arm, speech? (Immediate)
- [ ] Signs of severe allergic reaction? (Immediate)
- [ ] Severe uncontrolled bleeding? (Immediate)
- [ ] Patient under 28 days old with any fever? (Emergency — no exceptions)
- [ ] Patient expressing suicidal ideation? (Urgent — do not leave unaccompanied)
- [ ] Complaint does not match any routing rule? (Escalate to senior triage nurse)
