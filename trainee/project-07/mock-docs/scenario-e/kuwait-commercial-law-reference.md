# Kuwait Commercial Law — Reference Guide for Contract Review

## 1. Contract Formation Requirements

### 1.1 Offer and Acceptance

Under **Article 47 of Kuwait Commercial Law No. 68/1980**, a binding contract is formed when a definite offer is met by an unequivocal acceptance. Key requirements:

- The offer must be sufficiently definite as to subject matter, price (or mechanism for determining price), and the parties' respective obligations.
- Acceptance must mirror the offer in all material terms. A purported acceptance that introduces new or modified terms is treated as a counter-offer, not an acceptance (the "mirror image" rule).
- Under **Article 51**, an offer may be withdrawn at any time before acceptance, provided the withdrawal reaches the offeree before or simultaneously with the offer. Once the offeror has fixed a time limit for acceptance, the offer cannot be withdrawn within that period.
- **Article 53** provides that silence, standing alone, does not constitute acceptance in commercial contracts unless the parties have an established prior course of dealing or the nature of the transaction makes silence equivalent to acceptance.

### 1.2 Consideration

Kuwait commercial law, drawing on civil law tradition, does not require "consideration" in the English common law sense. Instead, **Article 61** requires that a contract have a **lawful cause (sabab)**. A contract whose cause is unlawful, contrary to public order, or contrary to public morals is void ab initio. Contracts that are gratuitous on one side may still be enforceable, but trainees should flag one-sided obligations for partner review.

### 1.3 Capacity

- **Article 39** requires that both parties have legal capacity to contract. Natural persons must have reached the age of majority (21 years under Kuwait civil law) and be of sound mind.
- Commercial companies must be duly incorporated and the signatory must be authorised to bind the entity. For Kuwaiti companies, authority derives from the articles of incorporation, board resolutions, or a duly issued power of attorney.
- **Article 42** provides that a contract concluded by an unauthorised representative is unenforceable against the principal unless subsequently ratified.

## 2. Enforceability Requirements

For a contract to be enforceable in Kuwait, the following conditions must be satisfied:

### 2.1 Lawfulness of Object

**Article 58** voids any contract whose object is impossible at the time of contracting, unlawful, or indeterminate to the extent that performance cannot be ascertained.

### 2.2 Written Form Requirements

- Certain categories of contract must be in writing to be enforceable. Under **Article 74**, contracts for the sale of real property, agency agreements, and long-term commercial leases (exceeding one year) must be in writing and, in several cases, notarised.
- Commercial contracts between merchants are enforceable whether written or oral, but **Article 76** provides that a written contract cannot be contradicted by oral testimony where the value exceeds KD 50 (note: this threshold was set in 1980; verify current monetary threshold with a supervisor).
- Contracts involving a government entity as a party must comply with Kuwait Government Tenders Law requirements and are typically subject to additional formalities.

### 2.3 Registration

Agency and distribution agreements with foreign principals must be registered with the Kuwait Chamber of Commerce and Industry under the Commercial Agents Law (**Law No. 36/1964 as amended**). An unregistered exclusive agency agreement may not be enforceable by the local agent against the foreign principal.

## 3. Penalty Clauses

### 3.1 General Rule

**Article 211 of Kuwait Commercial Law No. 68/1980** permits parties to agree in advance on a sum payable upon breach (a liquidated damages or penalty clause). Such a clause is binding on the courts.

### 3.2 Judicial Power to Modify

Critically, under **Article 213**, a Kuwait court retains discretion to **reduce a penalty clause** if:
- The obligor can demonstrate that the clause is grossly excessive relative to the actual damage suffered; or
- The obligor has partially performed the contract.

A court may also **increase** a penalty clause up to the level of actual damage if the obligee proves actual loss exceeding the stipulated amount.

### 3.3 Practical Implication for Trainees

Trainees reviewing contracts should:
1. Flag any penalty clause that appears punitive rather than compensatory — Kuwait courts will reduce it, which may not be in the client's interest if the client is the beneficiary of the penalty.
2. Flag the absence of a penalty clause in contracts where delay damages are important to the client — the client would then need to prove actual loss.
3. Note that penalty clauses in government contracts are subject to additional rules under procurement regulations.

## 4. Governing Law and Jurisdiction

### 4.1 Freedom of Choice

**Article 27 of the Kuwait Private International Law provisions** (incorporated into the Civil Code) permits contracting parties to choose the governing law of their agreement. This choice is generally respected by Kuwait courts, subject to the following limitations:

- Kuwait courts will not apply a chosen foreign law if its application would violate Kuwait public order or morals.
- Mandatory provisions of Kuwait law (including consumer protection rules, labour law protections, and rules applicable to Kuwait-registered companies) apply regardless of a choice of foreign law.

### 4.2 When Kuwait Courts Have Jurisdiction

Under **Article 31 of the Kuwait Code of Civil and Commercial Procedure**, Kuwait courts have jurisdiction where:
- The defendant is domiciled or habitually resident in Kuwait;
- The contract was entered into in Kuwait;
- The contract is to be performed in Kuwait; or
- The parties have agreed to submit to Kuwait jurisdiction.

A jurisdiction clause in favour of a foreign court is enforceable in Kuwait provided it is unambiguous, both parties had equal bargaining power, and it does not deprive a party of a mandatory right under Kuwait law.

### 4.3 Flag Points

Trainees should flag contracts where:
- The governing law clause and the jurisdiction clause point to different legal systems without explanation.
- A Kuwait-registered company purports to submit to exclusive foreign jurisdiction for obligations to be performed in Kuwait — this may be unenforceable in practice.
- There is no governing law clause at all (Kuwait courts will apply Kuwait law by default to contracts connected to Kuwait).

## 5. Arbitration Clauses

### 5.1 Legal Basis

Kuwait recognises arbitration under **Law No. 11/1995** (Arbitration in Civil and Commercial Matters) and subsequently through the adoption of rules aligned with the UNCITRAL Model Law. Kuwait is also a signatory to the New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards.

### 5.2 Requirements for a Valid Arbitration Clause

Under **Article 4 of Law No. 11/1995**, an arbitration agreement must:
- Be in writing (a clause within a broader written contract satisfies this requirement);
- Identify the subject matter of the disputes to be referred to arbitration with sufficient certainty; and
- Not purport to arbitrate disputes that are non-arbitrable under Kuwait law (e.g., matters of personal status, criminal liability, or disputes exclusively reserved for Kuwait courts by statute).

### 5.3 Non-Arbitrable Matters

Disputes involving government entities are subject to restriction — under **Article 7**, a Kuwait government body may only submit to arbitration with prior approval of the Council of Ministers. Trainees reviewing contracts with a government counterparty must verify whether such approval has been obtained.

### 5.4 Seat and Rules

Kuwait law does not mandate a specific seat or set of rules. Common choices in contracts with a Kuwait nexus include: Kuwait Chamber of Commerce Arbitration Centre, ICC, LCIA, and DIAC. The choice of seat affects the procedural law governing the arbitration.

## 6. Assignment of Contract Rights

### 6.1 General Rule

Under **Article 182**, contractual rights may be assigned to a third party without the consent of the obligor, unless the contract expressly prohibits assignment or the nature of the obligation makes assignment inappropriate (e.g., contracts based on personal skill or trust).

### 6.2 Notice Requirement

**Article 184** requires that notice of assignment be given to the obligor. Until notice is given, the obligor may validly perform to the original obligee and will be discharged. Trainees should confirm that assignment clauses:
- Specify whether assignment is permitted, restricted, or prohibited;
- Address whether consent is required (and from whom);
- Deal with assignment to affiliates separately from assignment to unrelated third parties (many standard forms permit intra-group assignment but require consent for external assignment).

### 6.3 Assignment of the Entire Contract

Assignment of the entire contract (both rights and obligations, i.e., novation) requires the consent of all parties under **Article 186**. A clause permitting assignment without qualification may not, on its own, permit a party to transfer its obligations.

## 7. Force Majeure

### 7.1 Statutory Basis

Kuwait law recognises force majeure (quwwa qahira) as a general doctrine under **Article 167 of the Kuwait Civil Code (Law No. 67/1980)**. A party is discharged from contractual liability if performance becomes impossible due to an external event that:
- Was unforeseeable at the time of contracting;
- Was unavoidable despite the exercise of reasonable care; and
- Was not caused by or attributable to the affected party.

### 7.2 Effect

Where force majeure is established, the affected obligation is extinguished — not merely suspended. If only part of the obligation is affected, the contract continues to apply to the remainder.

### 7.3 Contractual Force Majeure Clauses

Many commercial contracts include express force majeure clauses that define the triggering events and the consequences (suspension vs. termination). **Article 168** permits parties to expand or restrict the statutory definition, subject to public order. Trainees should flag:
- Force majeure clauses that are exceptionally narrow (e.g., excluding epidemic, government action, or labour disputes) where those risks are material;
- Clauses that provide indefinite suspension without a termination right — this can leave a party trapped indefinitely;
- Absence of a notice requirement — some clauses require the affected party to notify within a set period as a condition of relying on force majeure.

## 8. Contracts Involving Foreign Parties

### 8.1 Foreign Company Requirements

A foreign company wishing to contract in Kuwait for services to be performed in Kuwait must generally either:
- Establish a Kuwaiti presence (branch, subsidiary, or joint venture) in compliance with the Foreign Direct Investment Law (**Law No. 116/2013** as amended); or
- Engage a registered Kuwaiti commercial agent under the Commercial Agents Law (**Law No. 36/1964**).

Direct contracting by a foreign company without a local presence may be enforceable between the parties but can create regulatory and enforcement difficulties.

### 8.2 Currency and Payment

Under Exchange Control provisions, certain payments to foreign parties must be structured to comply with Central Bank of Kuwait regulations. Trainees should flag contracts where large payments are to be remitted abroad without any reference to applicable exchange control compliance.

### 8.3 Language

Kuwait law does not require commercial contracts to be in Arabic, but disputes adjudicated in Kuwait courts are conducted in Arabic. Contracts in English only may require certified translation. Where a bilingual contract contains inconsistencies, **Article 93 of the Civil Code** provides that the Arabic version prevails unless the parties have expressly agreed otherwise. Trainees should flag bilingual contracts where the language of precedence is not stated.
